  • Game

    Snake

    Eat apples, grow longer, and stay off the walls and your own tail.

    How it works

    The board is a grid and the snake is a list of cells. Every tick the head moves one cell and each body part takes the place of the one in front of it. Eating an apple keeps the old tail, which is how the snake grows. Turns are queued until the next move, so two quick key presses can never reverse it into itself. Play with the arrow keys, WASD or swipes.

  • Game

    Pong

    Table tennis against the computer or a friend on the same device.

    How it works

    The bounce angle depends on where the ball hits the paddle: near the edge sends it off steeply, the middle sends it straight back. The ball gets a little faster with every hit. The computer paddle follows the ball with a capped speed, so it can be beaten. First to 7 points wins.

  • Game

    Tic Tac Toe

    Three opponents, from random moves to one that never loses.

    How it works

    Easy plays random moves. Medium wins when it can and blocks your winning moves. Hard uses minimax with alpha-beta pruning: it plays out every possible game from the current board and picks the move with the best guaranteed result, so the best you can get is a draw.

  • Game

    Minesweeper

    Clear the board without hitting a mine. The first click is always safe.

    How it works

    The mines are placed after your first click, never on or next to it, so every game starts with an open area. Each number counts the mines in the eight cells around it. Opening a cell with no mines nearby opens its neighbours too, which spreads across empty regions. Clicking a number whose mines are all flagged opens the rest of its neighbours.

  • Simulation

    Game of Life

    Draw a few cells and watch Conway's automaton take over.

    How it works

    Each cell is alive or dead and looks at its eight neighbours. A live cell with two or three live neighbours survives, a dead cell with exactly three comes to life, and every other cell dies or stays empty. These two rules are enough to create gliders, oscillators and patterns that grow forever. The edges wrap around.

  • Simulation

    Boids

    Flocking birds from three local rules, with sliders for each one.

    How it works

    Every boid only sees its neighbours. It steers towards their average position (cohesion), away from boids that are too close (separation), and towards their average heading (alignment). Nobody leads the flock; the group behaviour emerges from these rules. Change their weights and watch the flock react.

  • Simulation

    Genetic Algorithm

    Dots learn to reach a goal past obstacles, one generation at a time.

    How it works

    Each dot has a "brain": a fixed list of random accelerations. When all dots stop, each gets a fitness score based on how close it got and how quickly it reached the goal. Fitter dots are more likely to be picked as parents, their brains are copied with small random mutations, and the best dot survives unchanged. Over generations the paths get shorter.

  • Algorithm

    QuadTree

    Find nearby points without checking every single one.

    How it works

    A quadtree splits a region into four quadrants whenever it holds too many points, and does it again inside each crowded quadrant. To find the points in a box, it skips every quadrant that doesn't overlap the box. The counter shows how many points were actually checked compared to the total.

  • Algorithm

    Maze

    A maze carved out step by step, then solved by three different searches.

    How it works

    The recursive backtracker walks to random unvisited cells and backs up when it gets stuck, which carves long winding corridors. Prim's algorithm grows the maze from random cells on its edge and leaves many short dead ends. The solvers share one loop and only differ in which waiting cell they explore next: the oldest (breadth- first), the newest (depth-first) or the most promising one (A*). The colors show the order of the search.

  • Algorithm

    Sorting

    Watch six sorting algorithms work, one comparison at a time.

    How it works

    Each algorithm is written as a generator that pauses after every comparison and every write, so the animation can show one step at a time. The counters show the difference: bubble, insertion and selection sort need around n² comparisons, while merge sort, quicksort and heapsort get by with about n log n.

  • Fractal

    Chaos Game

    Random jumps towards the corners of a polygon that draw a fractal.

    How it works

    Start anywhere, pick a random corner, move part of the way towards it and draw a dot. Repeat thousands of times. With a triangle and half the distance the dots form the Sierpinski triangle. Other corner counts, ratios and rules (like never picking the same corner twice) produce different fractals.

  • Fractal

    Barnsley Fern

    A realistic fern grown point by point from four simple formulas.

    How it works

    Each new point comes from the previous one through one of four affine transformations, picked at random with fixed probabilities. One draws the stem, one makes each leaf a smaller copy of the whole fern, and two create the lowest leaves on each side. The self-similar shape appears after a few thousand points.

  • Math

    Fourier Series

    Spinning circles stacked on each other add up to a square wave.

    How it works

    Any repeating wave can be written as a sum of sine waves. Each circle is one term of that sum, rotating at its own speed and size, attached to the tip of the previous circle. The height of the last tip, traced over time, draws the wave. More terms make sharper corners.

  • Fractal

    Fractal Trees

    Recursive trees with random colors, curves and blossoms.

    How it works

    A branch is drawn as a curve, then two shorter, thinner branches grow from its tip at slightly random angles, and each of those does the same. The recursion stops when branches get very short and draws a blossom instead. Every press of the button grows a new tree.

  • Fractal

    Mandelbrot

    Zoom into an endlessly detailed fractal made from one formula.

    How it works

    Every pixel stands for a number c. Starting from zero, the formula z = z² + c is repeated. If z stays small, the point belongs to the set and is drawn dark; if it escapes, the color shows how quickly. The image renders a few rows per frame so the page stays responsive, and the previous image is stretched into place while the next one renders.

  • Effect

    Particle Network

    Drifting dots linked by lines that move out of your way.

    How it works

    Particles bounce around the screen, and every pair closer than a set distance is joined by a line that fades as they move apart. Particles near the pointer are pushed away from it. A popular animated background for websites.

    Animated Clouds

    Hidden particles that puff up into clouds wherever you move.

    How it works

    A thousand tiny particles drift slowly across the screen. The ones near the pointer grow quickly, and they shrink back once it moves on, leaving a trail of clouds behind it.

  • Effect

    Spinning Stars

    Orbiting rings that leave long trails, like a star-trail photo.

    How it works

    Rings orbit the center at different distances and speeds. Instead of clearing the canvas, each frame is covered with an almost transparent black layer, so older positions fade slowly into trails.

  • Effect

    Particle Rain

    Falling particles reveal an image. Try it with your own photo.

    How it works

    The image is read pixel by pixel and its brightness is stored in a grid. Thousands of particles fall from the top; on bright pixels they slow down and glow, on dark ones they rush through. Where they linger, the picture shows up.

  • Effect

    Interactive Particles

    An image rebuilt from particles that scatter when you touch them.

    How it works

    Every visible pixel of a small image becomes a particle with a home position. Particles near the pointer are pushed away, harder the closer they are, and ease back home once it leaves. Upload your own image to try it on something else.

  • Effect

    Flow Field

    Thousands of particles drifting along an invisible current.

    How it works

    Perlin noise gives every point on the screen a smoothly changing direction. Thousands of particles follow those directions and each draws a short line every frame. The noise slowly changes over time, so the flow keeps shifting, and the pointer bends it into a whirlpool. Turn off fading to let the lines build up into a painting.
